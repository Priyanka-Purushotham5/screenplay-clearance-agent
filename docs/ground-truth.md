# Ground truth — `docs/test_screenplay.pdf`

The fixed answer key for the test screenplay. Every clearance-relevant item
in the document is listed here with the rating it should receive and the
reasoning behind that rating, so that a pipeline change can be *scored*
instead of eyeballed.

The machine-readable twin of this file is `docs/ground-truth.json`.
`api/scripts/verify_b7.py` checks both against the actual parsed screenplay
and against each other, so neither can drift away from the document or from
the other without a test going red.

> **Scope.** These ratings reflect ordinary production-clearance practice and
> are written to grade a hackathon pipeline. They are not legal advice, and
> real clearance is done by counsel with a script-clearance report and an E&O
> carrier. Where a call is genuinely contestable, this document says so rather
> than pretending to certainty.

## The three ratings

Defined by what they cost a production, not by how alarming they feel.

| Rating | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **RED** | Cannot be shot as written. Needs a signed licence or consent, or a rewrite. |
| **AMBER** | Can be shot, but generates a clearance action — a release, a placement deal, an art-department substitution, or a line in the E&O report. |
| **GREEN** | No action required. |

The dividing line between AMBER and GREEN is whether anyone has to *do*
something. The line between AMBER and RED is whether the production can
proceed while they do it.

than discovered.

## Discrimination sets — the part that matters

Rating each item correctly in isolation is the easy half. What proves the
pipeline is reading *context* rather than matching entity names is that the
same entity gets different ratings in different places.

| Set | Entity | Members | Must span |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Take On Me | gt_01, gt_02, gt_17 | RED, GREEN |
| **B** | Coca-Cola | gt_03, gt_04, gt_05, gt_21, gt_22 | AMBER, GREEN, RED |
| **C** | Nobu | gt_11, gt_12 | AMBER, GREEN |

A system that gives both members of a set the same rating has failed, even
if its overall per-item accuracy looks respectable. Uniform ratings within a
set are the signature of name matching, and no aggregate score will surface
that on its own.

Set C exists to check that the action-versus-dialogue rule *generalises*.
It would be easy to hard-code "songs in dialogue are fine" and score well on
set A while understanding nothing.

## Reasoning, item by item

### gt_01 — "Take On Me" playing on a turntable · Scene 1 · action · **RED**

> On a battered turntable, 'Take On Me' by a-ha plays loudly

A named commercial recording audible in the scene needs **two** clearances,
which is the thing most people get wrong: a **synchronisation licence** from
the publisher for the composition, and a **master use licence** from the
label for that particular recording. Either can be refused outright.

The action line pins the specific recording — "that unmistakable synth riff"
— so re-recording it with session musicians, the usual way out of a master
use fee, would not deliver what the scene asks for without a rewrite.

*Action:* obtain sync + master use, or rewrite to an unnamed or library cue.

### gt_02 — "Take On Me" named in dialogue · Scene 2 · dialogue · **GREEN**

> You know that song? Take On Me? I actually love that song.

A character speaking a song title is not a use of the song. Titles are too
short to carry copyright on their own, and no recording is reproduced.
Nothing to licence.

The boundary is **lyrics**. Had the line quoted even one line of the lyric,
this flips to RED and needs a publisher licence — and unlike sync, lyric
quotation is often refused on principle. The gap between "names the song"
and "quotes the song" is the whole finding.

### gt_03 — Coca-Cola can, logo to camera · Scene 1 · action · **AMBER**

> A Coca-Cola can sits beside it, logo facing the lens.

Trademark law targets confusion as to source, not depiction, so a real
product sitting on a set is ordinarily unremarkable. What lifts this above
incidental is the deliberate framing: "logo facing the lens" is the language
of product placement, and it invites either a placement agreement or a note
from legal to turn the can around.

*Action:* placement agreement, or dress with a generic and turn the label away.

### gt_04 — Coca-Cola in a business conversation · Scene 3 · dialogue · **GREEN**

> He says the Coca-Cola deal is dead.

Nominative reference to a real company inside a conversation about business.
Neutral, no product claim, nothing depicted. This is ordinary speech about
the commercial world; requiring permission for it would make most dialogue
unwritable.

### gt_05 — Coca-Cola disparaged · Scene 6 · dialogue · **RED**

> Coca-Cola tastes like syrup and battery acid. It rots your teeth out of
> your head and they put a polar bear on the can so nobody notices.

This is neither depiction nor reference. A named brand is attacked, and the
attack carries a **factual-sounding health claim** stated as fact rather than
framed as opinion, which is what moves the exposure from trademark
tarnishment alone into trade libel territory. The line also invokes the polar
bear, an advertising character belonging to the same owner.

No placement deal will be offered for this, and the brand will object. The
standard fix is to invent a brand before shooting.

*Action:* rename to a fictional brand, or cut the product claim and keep the
line clearly in the register of opinion.

**Compare with gt_03 and gt_04.** Same entity, three contexts, three answers.
This is the strongest single test in the document.

### gt_06 — Pepsi named in the same line · Scene 3 · dialogue · **GREEN**

> They found out about the Pepsi placement last quarter.

Identical analysis to gt_04. It is here so extraction is tested on **two
distinct entities inside one element**, which is where span-based extractors
tend to return only the first and stop.

### gt_07 — WEA International on a prop label · Scene 1 · action · **AMBER**

> The label reads: WEA International.

A real Warner-owned label name, legible on a prop. Same category as gt_03 but
far less prominent — which is exactly why it earns its place. A rubric that
cannot separate "legible on a prop" from "held to the lens" will over-rate one
or under-rate the other, and having both in the document makes that visible.

### gt_08 — Nielsen named in dialogue · Scene 1 · dialogue · **GREEN**

> The Nielsen rep is here. And... he brought lawyers.

A real company named neutrally in dialogue. It is listed because the
extraction agent does pick it up, and an answer key that stays silent about a
real extracted entity cannot distinguish a false positive from a correct find.

### gt_09 — *Nighthawks* on the wall · Scene 2 · action · **RED**

> On the wall behind her hangs Nighthawks by Edward Hopper, a large print in
> a thin black frame.

Painted 1942; Hopper died 1967. Protected in the United States as a published
1942 work with renewal, and in life-plus-70 territories until 2038 — the two
terms happen to land in the same year.

The script hangs "a large print" on the wall, so the **whole work** is
reproduced, prominently, and for as long as the scene runs. That is not
de minimis, which is the doctrine that saves a poster glimpsed out of focus
in the background.

*Action:* licence the reproduction, or replace with cleared or public-domain art.

### gt_10 — *Guernica* on the wall · Scene 4 · action · **RED**

> A framed poster of Guernica by Pablo Picasso dominates the far wall.

Painted 1937; Picasso died 1973, so life-plus-70 runs to 2043, and the estate
both licenses and enforces actively. "Dominates the far wall" makes it
prominent and sustained.

A second artwork exists so that a rubric cannot pass this category by
special-casing one famous title.

### gt_11 — Nobu as a location · Scene 3 · action · **AMBER**

> They pass a brightly lit Nobu restaurant

A real, named, operating business, identifiable on screen. A passing exterior
in a driving shot sits close to incidental, but the name is scripted and the
frontage is trade dress, so production wants a location agreement if shooting
there and a name change if not.

### gt_12 — Nobu named in dialogue · Scene 5 · dialogue · **GREEN**

> Meet me at Nobu. Twenty minutes.

Naming a real restaurant in dialogue depicts nothing and implies no
association. Same distinction as gt_01 versus gt_02, transposed from music to
premises — which is the point. The rule either generalises or it does not.

### gt_13 — Shakespeare quoted · Scene 3 · dialogue · **GREEN**

> To be, or not to be -- that is the question.

*Hamlet*, c.1600, public domain everywhere. No licence, no attribution
requirement.

The trap is that this is unmistakably a quotation from a famous literary
work, and a tool that flags "quotes a literary work" will rate it RED. Age of
the work is the entire answer — and it is the *same* reasoning that makes
gt_09 and gt_10 RED. A pipeline that gets all three right is applying a term
calculation; one that gets this wrong while getting the paintings right is
probably keyword-matching "painting" and "quote".

### gt_14 — Taylor Swift named in dialogue · Scene 7 · dialogue · **AMBER**

> Taylor Swift re-recorded her first six albums when she couldn't buy the
> masters back.

A living public figure named in dialogue. The statement is true, a matter of
public record, non-defamatory, and implies no endorsement, which places it
inside ordinary expressive use and the newsworthiness exception to right of
publicity.

It is AMBER rather than GREEN for two practical reasons: the name carries
commercial value, and **E&O insurers routinely require a real-person report on
any named living individual** before they will bind coverage. Paperwork, not a
blocker.

*Contestable.* A reasonable rubric could call this GREEN on the law alone. It
is graded AMBER because the deliverable is a clearance report, and a clearance
report that omits a named living person is incomplete regardless of how the
law comes out.

### gt_15 — Marcus Harman · Scene 4 · action · **GREEN** *(evidence required)*

> MARCUS HARMAN (60s, silver-haired, the actual music industry executive)

Marcus Harman is invented. The parenthetical "the actual music industry
executive" is bait: it asserts, *inside the fiction*, that the character is
real.

The correct answer is that no such public figure exists and there is nothing
to clear. But the correct **behaviour** is to establish that by searching
rather than by assuming, because this failure mode is silent — an agent that
guesses "fictional" and an agent that checks produce identical output on this
document, and only one of them is doing clearance.

*The finding must cite the search.* A GREEN here with no evidence should not
score as a pass.

### gt_16 — Dr. James Holloway · Scene 4 · dialogue · **GREEN** *(evidence required)*

> Your client Dr. James Holloway signed a personal guarantee.

The hard one. A fictional character with a plausible real name, a real
profession, and an unflattering depiction: a debtor disputing a personal
guarantee. This is the textbook name-clearance case, because a real and
identifiable Dr. James Holloway could bring a defamation claim on exactly
these facts.

GREEN is **conditional** on a name search finding no identifiable individual
in the same field, and an honest finding recommends a formal name clearance
anyway.

*Action:* name clearance search. If a real match surfaces in the same
profession, change the name.

### gt_17 — "Turn that off" · Scene 1 · dialogue · **RED**

> Of course he did. Turn that off.

Diana says this about the record playing on the turntable two lines earlier.
"That" is the song — so this is the *same on-screen use* as gt_01, not a
reference to it, and it needs no separate licence and gets no separate pass.

It is in set A deliberately, because it is the counterexample to the rule a
lazy implementation would learn from gt_01 and gt_02: *action line = RED,
dialogue = GREEN*. That rule is wrong, and this line is where it breaks. What
determines the rating is whether the work is **playing**, not which element
type the words sit in. A pipeline that rates gt_02 GREEN and gt_17 GREEN has
learned the wrong rule and will be right for the wrong reason on every
screenplay after this one.

### gt_21 — Coca-Cola bottle in the diner · Scene 6 · action · **AMBER**

> Felix slides a Coca-Cola bottle across the Formica.

A named brand physically handled on screen, with no framing direction — the
quiet end of depiction, well below gt_03's "logo facing the lens".

Still AMBER rather than GREEN, because the clearance action is the same one:
a placement agreement, or the art department swaps in a generic. Prominence
changes the negotiation, not whether there is one. Having both in the document
tests whether a rubric can tell loud depiction from quiet depiction without
collapsing either into "mention".

## Scored neither way

| Quote | Scene | Why |

| "across the Formica" | 6 | Formica is a registered trademark, used here generically for a countertop surface rather than depicted as a branded product. It was not planted deliberately — it turned up when scene 6 was written, and it is recorded here because it is genuinely borderline. Flagging it AMBER is defensible; missing it is defensible. |

## How this is used

`verify_b7.py` validates the key itself. Downstream, the assessment stage is
scored against it:

- **Exact rating match** — full credit.
- **Adjacent miss** (AMBER↔GREEN, AMBER↔RED) — partial. The item was noticed
  and the severity was misjudged.
- **Inversion** (RED↔GREEN) — zero, and the failure that matters. Something
  was either missed entirely or waved through.
- **Set discrimination** — pass/fail per set, independent of item scores. All
  members of a set carrying the same rating is a failure of the set.
- **Evidence** — entries marked `requires_evidence` need a cited search, not
  just the right letter.

## Maintenance

The screenplay and this key are one artefact in two files. If
`api/scripts/make_test_pdf.py` changes:

1. Regenerate: `python api/scripts/make_test_pdf.py`
2. Re-run `verify_b7.py` — quotes that no longer appear fail loudly, which is
   the point of anchoring on quoted text rather than on element numbers.
3. Update this file and `ground-truth.json` **together**; `verify_b7.py`
   compares the table above against the JSON and fails if they disagree.

Append new scenes at the **end**. Inserting one renumbers every element below
it, and `api/app/agents/fixtures/expected_elements.json` pins element ids.
